Level 3 · Research Mathematics & Applied Statistical Testing

Phase: Research Foundations & RA Onboarding
Suggested duration: 13 sessions (~3 weeks)
Prerequisites: Level 2 (Scholarly Literature Search, Paper Reading & Reference Management)
Scope: Master the essential mathematical apparatus and statistical hypothesis testing methods required for Computer Science and Data Science research: linear algebra decompositions (SVD, PCA, spectral graph theory), multivariate calculus and autograd foundations, probability theory, parametric vs. non-parametric hypothesis tests (Student's t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Mann-Whitney U), statistical significance vs. effect sizes (Cohen's d), bootstrapping confidence intervals, and multiple hypothesis testing corrections (Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR) for rigorous paper reporting.

M3.1 · Linear Algebra & Spectral Methods for High-Dimensional Data — (sessions: 2)
Session 1 · Matrix Decompositions & Dimensionality Reduction
- Derive and compute Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and Eigendecomposition for high-dimensional feature matrices and covariance representations.
- Connect Principal Component Analysis (PCA) directly to SVD, analyzing variance explained and reconstruction error across truncated singular values.
- Apply low-rank matrix approximations (Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem) to compress neural network weight matrices and analyze latent representation ranks.
Session 2 · Vector Spaces, Norms & Spectral Graph Foundations
- Analyze matrix norms (spectral norm, Frobenius norm, nuclear norm) and their role in regularizing deep neural networks and bounding Lipschitz constants.
- Construct Graph Laplacian matrices (unnormalized, symmetric normalized, random walk) and compute graph spectra for relational and topological data.
- Relate graph eigenvalues and eigenvectors to connectivity, spectral clustering, and frequency filtering in spatial graph neural networks.
Mental model: SVD decomposes complex high-dimensional transformations into orthogonal coordinate rotations and scaling axes, revealing intrinsic data dimensionality.
Try: Implement SVD from scratch in NumPy, project a high-dimensional dataset onto its top 3 singular vectors, and calculate the cumulative explained variance ratio.
Outcome: Apply fundamental matrix decompositions, compute spectral graph invariants, and mathematically justify dimensionality reduction in research papers.

M3.2 · Multivariate Calculus, Optimization Geometry & Curvature — (sessions: 2)
Session 1 · Gradients, Jacobians & Computational Autodiff
- Calculate vector-valued partial derivatives, Jacobians, and gradient vectors for multi-layer composite matrix functions using multivariate chain rules.
- Connect symbolic multivariate calculus to algorithmic reverse-mode automatic differentiation (backpropagation) and vector-Jacobian products (VJPs).
- Formulate Taylor series approximations to first and second orders, analyzing local linearizations of complex non-linear loss surfaces.
Session 2 · Hessian Matrices, Loss Landscapes & Curvature
- Derive the Hessian matrix of second-order partial derivatives and analyze its eigenvalues to evaluate local curvature (convexity, saddle points, ravines).
- Calculate the condition number of the Hessian to diagnose optimization pathologies such as ill-conditioned gradient oscillations.
- Understand empirical loss landscape visualization techniques (filter normalization, contour projections) used to analyze neural network trainability.
Mental model: The gradient indicates the direction of steepest ascent, while the Hessian eigenvalues determine whether the terrain is a flat basin, a steep ravine, or an unstable saddle point.
Try: Compute the Hessian matrix of a non-linear loss function analytically and numerically, verify positive semi-definiteness, and compute its condition number.
Outcome: Analyze the geometric curvature of loss surfaces, derive custom matrix derivatives, and diagnose numerical optimization difficulties in deep learning models.

M3.3 · Probability Distributions & Information-Theoretic Foundations — (sessions: 2)
Session 1 · Multivariate Distributions & Maximum Likelihood
- Master parametric multivariate probability distributions: Gaussian, Bernoulli, Dirichlet, and categorical representations in generative and discriminative modeling.
- Derive Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) parameter estimates, linking priors directly to L1 (Laplace) and L2 (Gaussian) regularization.
- Analyze properties of exponential families, sufficiency, natural parameters, and log-partition functions in probabilistic graphical models.
Session 2 · Information Theory & Divergence Metrics
- Derive Shannon Entropy, Conditional Entropy, and Mutual Information as formal measures of uncertainty and information transfer between random variables.
- Compute Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence and Jensen-Shannon Divergence, understanding asymmetry and zero-forcing versus zero-avoiding behaviors.
- Link Cross-Entropy loss directly to empirical KL divergence minimization in classification and language modeling tasks.
Mental model: Minimizing cross-entropy is mathematically equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence between true empirical data and your model's predicted distribution.
Try: Implement KL divergence and Jensen-Shannon divergence between two multivariate Gaussian distributions and plot the divergence as variance parameters change.
Outcome: Apply formal information-theoretic metrics, formulate probabilistic model objectives, and interpret loss functions from first principles.

M3.4 · Parametric & Non-Parametric Hypothesis Testing in ML Experiments — (sessions: 2)
Session 1 · Testing Normality & Parametric Comparisons
- Understand why reporting raw mean accuracy without hypothesis testing fails peer review standards in top-tier conferences (NeurIPS, ICML, CVPR).
- Test experimental result distributions for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, D'Agostino-Pearson omnibus test, and Q-Q normality plots.
- Apply Student's paired two-tailed t-test when normality assumptions hold, calculating test statistics, degrees of freedom, and two-tailed p-values.
Session 2 · Non-Parametric Rank Tests & Multi-Model Comparisons
- Understand why non-parametric tests are required when evaluating machine learning benchmarks with non-Gaussian, skewed, or bounded metric distributions.
- Execute the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired algorithmic comparisons across multiple benchmark datasets or cross-validation folds.
- Apply the Mann-Whitney U test for independent sample comparisons and the Friedman test with post-hoc Nemenyi tests for comparing multiple algorithms simultaneously.
Mental model: A p-value does not measure superiority; it measures how rarely you would observe such performance divergence if both algorithms were identical.
Try: Take validation results from two model architectures across 10 random seeds, run Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality, and execute paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.
Outcome: Select and execute appropriate parametric and non-parametric hypothesis tests to statistically validate performance improvements over baseline models.

M3.5 · Effect Sizes, Confidence Intervals & Multiple Testing Correction — (sessions: 2)
Session 1 · Beyond p-Values: Effect Sizes & Bootstrapping
- Differentiate between statistical significance (low p-value) and practical scientific significance (meaningful performance gain in domain contexts).
- Calculate standardized effect size metrics: Cohen's d for parametric samples and Cliff's Delta or rank-biserial correlation for non-parametric comparisons.
- Implement empirical bootstrapping (10,000 resamples) to compute non-parametric 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) for complex evaluation metrics (e.g., F1, AUROC, BLEU).
Session 2 · Multiple Testing Corrections & p-Hacking Prevention
- Understand the multiple comparison problem: running 20 independent statistical tests at alpha = 0.05 guarantees an expected false positive rate of 64%.
- Implement conservative family-wise error rate control using the Bonferroni correction and Holm-Bonferroni step-down adjustments.
- Apply False Discovery Rate (FDR) control using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for large-scale ablation studies and multi-metric benchmark evaluations.
Mental model: Reporting a 95% bootstrap confidence interval communicates both the magnitude of improvement and the empirical certainty of the measurement.
Try: Write a Python utility that takes benchmark scores across 15 sub-tasks, computes Cohen's d effect sizes, and applies Benjamini-Hochberg FDR adjustments.
Outcome: Report effect sizes alongside confidence intervals and eliminate false positive claims caused by uncorrected multiple statistical comparisons.

M3.6 · Capstone Project: Statistical Rigor & Hypothesis Testing Audit Package — (sessions: 3)
Session 1 · Automated Significance Engine & Hypothesis Testing Pipeline
- Build an open-source Python statistical evaluation library tailored for machine learning and data science researchers.
- Ingest paired experimental runs across multiple seeds, datasets, and benchmark baselines from CSV, JSON, or Weights & Biases export formats.
- Automatically test distributional assumptions (normality, equal variance via Levene's test) and dynamically dispatch to paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.
Session 2 · Effect Sizes, Bootstrap Intervals & LaTeX Table Generation
- Compute 10,000-sample bootstrap confidence intervals and Cohen's d effect sizes for all reported evaluation metrics.
- Apply Benjamini-Hochberg corrections across multi-task evaluations, reporting adjusted p-values alongside raw values.
- Automatically generate publication-ready LaTeX tables with mean ± 95% CI formatting and asterisks indicating verified statistical significance thresholds.
Mental model: Reviewers respect papers where every performance claim is backed by formal confidence intervals, effect sizes, and statistical hypothesis tests.
Try: Execute the audit package on a simulated research experiment with 5 baselines and 10 seeds, generating a formatted LaTeX table ready for paper insertion.
Outcome: Possess an automated statistical verification toolkit that ensures all experimental claims in your papers adhere to rigorous scientific standards.

CAPSTONE PROJECT: Statistical Rigor & Hypothesis Testing Audit Package
BRIEF: Develop an automated, publication-ready Python statistical evaluation package that ingests benchmark results from multiple random seeds and baseline models, verifies distribution assumptions, computes parametric and non-parametric hypothesis tests, calculates bootstrap confidence intervals and effect sizes, and exports formatted LaTeX tables for academic submissions.
Build a statistical verification engine that:
- Ingests experimental result logs (e.g., accuracy, loss, F1, AUROC) across N random seeds for baseline and proposed methods.
- Performs automated normality screening using Shapiro-Wilk and Anderson-Darling tests, generating visual Q-Q plots.
- Dynamically executes paired Student's t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests based on verified normality criteria.
- Computes standardized effect sizes including Cohen's d and Cliff's Delta to quantify practical significance.
- Implements non-parametric empirical bootstrapping (10,000 iterations) to derive robust 95% confidence intervals.
- Corrects for multiple testing across multiple benchmarks using Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate procedures.
- Generates publication-grade LaTeX tables where statistically significant gains (p < 0.05, p < 0.01) are automatically annotated with standard significance markers.
Deliverables:
- Standalone Python package (statistical_audit/) with comprehensive docstrings, type hints, and unit test suite.
- Jupyter demonstration notebook evaluating two competing deep learning models across 10 random seeds on benchmark datasets.
- Automatically generated publication-ready LaTeX table comparing baseline vs proposed method with mean ± 95% CI.
- Diagnostic visualization dashboard plotting empirical distributions, bootstrap confidence bands, and Q-Q normality lines.
Outcome: You can statistically validate any empirical AI/ML research finding, guaranteeing that published results are mathematically sound and reproducible.
