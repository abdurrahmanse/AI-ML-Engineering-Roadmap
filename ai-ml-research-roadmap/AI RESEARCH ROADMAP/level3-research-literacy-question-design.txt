Level 3 · Research Literacy and Problem Formulation

Phase: Research Foundations & Lab Onboarding
Suggested duration: 26 sessions (~6 weeks)
Prerequisites: Level 2
Scope: Learn how research questions are discovered, evaluated, scoped, and communicated before a model is selected.

M3.1 · Finding and Reading Research Papers — (sessions: 5)
- Search scholarly databases, follow citation graphs, and record inclusion and exclusion decisions.
- Read papers in passes: claim and structure, method and evidence, then limitations and reproduction details.
- Maintain a literature matrix containing question, data, method, comparison, result, and unresolved issue.
- Use a literature matrix, citation graph, and claim-evidence table to distinguish established results from open questions.
- Write a preregistered research plan with primary outcomes, alternative explanations, stop conditions, and an analysis plan.
Mental model: A paper is an argument supported by evidence, not a list of fashionable techniques.
Try: Read five papers on one question and create a one-page evidence map with disagreements.
Outcome: You can find relevant literature and explain what a paper actually establishes.

M3.2 · Questions, Hypotheses & Research Designs — (sessions: 5)
- Turn a broad interest into a specific, measurable, falsifiable research question.
- Define variables, estimands, baselines, success criteria, and plausible alternative explanations.
- Match exploratory, observational, experimental, and qualitative designs to the question.
- Use a literature matrix, citation graph, and claim-evidence table to distinguish established results from open questions.
- Write a preregistered research plan with primary outcomes, alternative explanations, stop conditions, and an analysis plan.
Mental model: Good research narrows uncertainty by making competing explanations testable.
Try: Convert an interesting model idea into three hypotheses and a preregistered experiment outline.
Outcome: You can formulate research questions that are feasible, meaningful, and testable.

M3.3 · Reproducibility, Ethics & Collaboration — (sessions: 4)
- Use research logs, data statements, code review, preregistration, and artifact checklists.
- Explain informed consent, privacy, intellectual property, authorship, conflicts of interest, and dual use.
- Give and receive feedback using a claim-evidence-limitation structure.
- Use a literature matrix, citation graph, and claim-evidence table to distinguish established results from open questions.
- Write a preregistered research plan with primary outcomes, alternative explanations, stop conditions, and an analysis plan.
Mental model: Trust is an engineering property built through transparent choices and accountable collaboration.
Try: Review a peer's project proposal and request evidence for every major claim.
Outcome: You can participate in a research group with ethical judgment and reproducible habits.

M3.4 · Scholarly Search, Synthesis and Citation Practice — (sessions: 3)
- Search scholarly databases with controlled vocabulary, citation chaining, and explicit inclusion criteria.
- Synthesize disagreements by comparing claims, data, methods, assumptions, and evidence instead of counting citations.
Mental model: A literature review is an argument about evidence, not a catalogue of paper summaries.
Try: Build a claim-evidence matrix for one research question and identify three unresolved tensions.
Outcome: You can explain the state of knowledge and the gap your study addresses.

M3.5 · Research Proposal, Preregistration and Ethics — (sessions: 3)
- Write hypotheses, estimands, primary outcomes, risks, analysis choices, and decision gates before data collection.
- Identify consent, privacy, authorship, conflicts, dual use, and responsible-release requirements.
Mental model: A proposal is a contract between the question, the evidence, and the people affected by the study.
Try: Have a peer challenge your protocol and record every revision and reason.
Outcome: You can start a study with a feasible, ethical, and reviewable protocol.

M3.6 · Capstone: Literature Review and Research Proposal — (sessions: 6)
- Select a tractable data-science question and synthesize at least ten relevant sources.
- Write a proposal containing motivation, gap, hypotheses, data plan, methods, evaluation, risks, and timeline.
- Defend the proposal in a short presentation and revise it from recorded feedback.
Final result: A research proposal that turns a beginner's interest into a feasible, evidence-aware study.
