Level 1 · Mathematics for Data Science

Phase: Research Foundations & Lab Onboarding
Suggested duration: 26 sessions (~6 weeks)
Prerequisites: Level 0
Scope: Develop the algebra, functions, calculus, and linear algebra intuition required to understand models instead of treating libraries as magic.

M1.1 · Algebra, Functions & Logarithms — (sessions: 4)
- Manipulate equations, ratios, inequalities, powers, logarithms, and summations.
- Read a function as an input-output rule and interpret slope, curvature, and scale.
- Use units, orders of magnitude, and dimensional checks to catch impossible results.
- Use dimensional analysis, numerical checks, and visualizations to catch incorrect derivations before they enter an experiment.
- Connect each mathematical object to a modeling decision, an optimization behavior, and a measurable research consequence.
Mental model: Mathematical notation is a compact language for describing relationships that code can execute.
Try: Implement and plot linear, polynomial, exponential, and logarithmic functions, then explain where each is useful.
Outcome: You can translate common quantitative statements between words, equations, plots, and Python.

M1.2 · Linear Algebra & Geometry — (sessions: 5)
- Represent vectors, matrices, tensors, basis changes, dot products, norms, and projections.
- Derive matrix multiplication dimensions and use geometric language to explain distance and similarity.
- Implement a small neural layer with NumPy and verify it against hand-calculated examples.
- Use dimensional analysis, numerical checks, and visualizations to catch incorrect derivations before they enter an experiment.
- Connect each mathematical object to a modeling decision, an optimization behavior, and a measurable research consequence.
Mental model: Most machine-learning computation is geometry performed efficiently on batches of examples.
Try: Build a visual notebook for projections, least squares, principal components, and cosine similarity.
Outcome: You can follow the linear-algebra operations behind regression, embeddings, and neural networks.

M1.3 · Derivatives and Optimization Intuition — (sessions: 5)
- Interpret derivatives, partial derivatives, gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians.
- Apply the chain rule to a small computation graph and check gradients numerically.
- Explain learning rate, local minima, conditioning, and why normalization affects optimization.
- Use dimensional analysis, numerical checks, and visualizations to catch incorrect derivations before they enter an experiment.
- Connect each mathematical object to a modeling decision, an optimization behavior, and a measurable research consequence.
Mental model: Training adjusts parameters in directions that reduce a chosen error surface.
Try: Implement gradient descent for a one-variable and two-variable function and compare step sizes.
Outcome: You can explain and verify the calculus used to train predictive models.

M1.4 · Matrix Calculus and Automatic Differentiation — (sessions: 3)
- Express Jacobians, Hessians, vector-Jacobian products, and matrix derivatives with correct shapes.
- Verify an automatic-differentiation result with finite differences and diagnose numerical error.
Mental model: Shape checks and independent derivatives are mathematical unit tests.
Try: Derive and verify gradients for a two-layer network with a scalar loss.
Outcome: You can inspect the calculus behind a training implementation.

M1.5 · Optimization Algorithms and Numerical Stability — (sessions: 3)
- Compare batch, stochastic, and mini-batch gradient descent under different conditioning and step sizes.
- Explain exploding updates, vanishing gradients, normalization, regularization, and stable log-domain computation.
Mental model: Optimization is an experiment whose assumptions and failure modes must be measured.
Try: Compare optimizers on a controlled loss surface and report convergence, sensitivity, and failure cases.
Outcome: You can connect mathematical assumptions to optimization behavior.

M1.6 · Capstone: Mathematical Model Notebook — (sessions: 6)
- Derive ordinary least squares from a clearly stated objective and assumptions.
- Implement the solution with NumPy and compare it with a trusted library.
- Include plots, dimensional checks, numerical-gradient checks, and a plain-language explanation.
Final result: A mathematical notebook that connects equations, geometry, optimization, code, and model behavior.
